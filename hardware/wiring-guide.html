Power and ground

Each input board's 5V pin is raw USB VBUS with no diode on the board, so each one needs its own Schottky before the shared rail. Without them you would tie your two hosts' 5 V supplies together.

The rail is not 5 V

It is the host's VBUS minus a cable drop minus one Schottky drop: about 4.25–5.2 V, typically ~4.65 V. Everything downstream is specified for that. Measure it on the built box before trusting the estimate.

Figure 3

I2S — clocks out, audio in

The ESP32 generates both clocks and sends them to both input boards, which are pure slaves. It also feeds those same two clocks back into two of its own input-only pins, because its second I2S peripheral has to run as a slave on them.

Figure 4

UART — the control link

One serial link per input board, carrying commands between the configuration web page and the ESP32. These are the only wires that cross from the back of the ESP32 to the input boards, so route them around the ends rather than through the middle of the loom.

Perfboard layout

A single strip across the service gap, between the input boards and the ESP32. It carries the two things that need components — the power rail and the clock fan-out. Audio data and UART stay point-to-point, because routing them through here would add twelve solder joints and buy nothing.

21 × 5 holes at 0.1″, cut to about 54 × 13 mm. Plain perfboard, not stripboard: holes are isolated, and the two buses are bare tinned wire laid along the top. Every axial part stands upright, which keeps the strip shallow and uniform.

Build it in this order

  • Buses first, while the board is empty. GND bare wire along row 3, columns 1–11. RAIL along row 4, columns 1–11. BCLK along row 1, columns 13–16. LRCK along row 1, columns 18–21. Solder each at every column it crosses.
  • Then the two diodes. Both upright, banded end down into the RAIL bus at c1 r4 and c3 r4. The plain ends sit in row 5 where the input boards' 5V wires arrive.
  • Then the four resistors, upright, hanging from the two clock buses on row 1 down into row 2.
  • Then the capacitors. C1 at column 8 and C2 at column 10, each straddling rows 3 and 4. C1's + leg goes in row 4.
  • Wires last, by the numbers above. Leave them long; trim on final assembly.

What this costs the box

The strip needs a 16 mm service gap instead of the current 6 mm, and about 1.5 mm more headroom for the upright parts. The enclosure grows from 64.6 × 79.7 × 15.2 mm to roughly 64.6 × 89.7 × 17.2 mm — 10 mm deeper, 2 mm taller. The footprint's width does not change.

Also: keep the 22 µF under 8 mm tall, or it will not clear the lid. A 5 × 7 mm radial, a tantalum or a 1206 ceramic all work; the common 5 × 11 mm electrolytic does not.

With JST connectors: the perfboard becomes a backplane

Putting a connector on every board changes what the perfboard is for. It stops being a component carrier that a few wires pass by, and becomes the hub every signal routes through — because a signal can only reach a connector if it lands on that board first.

That is a real change from Figure 5, where audio data and UART stayed point-to-point. Now all twenty-five wires terminate on the hub, in four cables: one per board, plus a second for the ESP32 because its two headers face opposite directions.

What this buys and what it costs

You can lift any board outUnplug one connector and the board comes free. Today every board is soldered into the loom, so removing one means desoldering seven joints.
It cannot be plugged in backwardsXH housings are keyed. J1 and J2 are both 7-way and will swap, but that only swaps which host is "input 1" — harmless. Label them anyway.
25 crimpsBuy pre-crimped XH leads rather than crimping by hand; you push them into the housings and solder the free ends to the boards.
Six extra links on the hubAudio and UART now pass through the board with no component in the way, so each needs an underside jumper.

Vertical headers cost height and keep the box shorter; right-angle headers cost a lot of depth because the mating plug needs room in the same plane. Vertical is the better trade here. These are estimates from nominal XH dimensions — measure your actual connectors before I cut the model.

Pitch: 2.50 mm connector, 2.54 mm perfboard

JST XH is 2.50 mm. Across a 7-way header that is 0.24 mm of accumulated error, which the pins absorb without complaint — people do this constantly. It is worth knowing before you wonder why the last pin feels tight. If you want an exact fit instead, a 2.54 mm latching connector (Molex KK-254 and its clones) drops straight in.

Check these against the real boards

  • Pin order along the ESP32's headers. Every GPIO number here comes from Waveshare's schematic and is solid, but the drawings place the stubs for readability, not in true header order. Find each pin by its silkscreen label.
  • The ESP32's power pin marking. The schematic net is VSYS; the silkscreen may read 5V. The input boards' equivalent pin is definitely silkscreened 5V while its net is also called VSYS. Same thing in all cases: raw supply, not regulated 3.3 V.
  • The second USB-C port on each input board sits on that same raw-supply net, on the host side of the diode. It is sealed inside the box — leave it that way.
  • Leave slack. The ESP32 is screwed to the base and the lid only presses the input boards, so opening the box does not pull on the loom. It still helps to leave enough wire to lift the lid clear and set it down beside the base.
